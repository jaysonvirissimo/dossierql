# Novice walkthrough of The Shadow Network

## Preflight and outcome

I started at clearance 1 with 0 of 10 cases resolved. I played as a junior programmer who knows JSON, has followed an API tutorial, and has never written GraphQL. I read the optional Orientation, passed all six training drills in six transmissions, and then followed the campaign in assignment order. I used the in-game starter queries, Documentation, Glossary, and debriefs.

The assignments desk ended at clearance 6 with 10 of 10 cases resolved. Every case recorded an independent completion. I took no hints, viewed no solution before resolving its case, and never ran out of time. I read reference queries in the debriefs after solving. The game counted 17 campaign transmissions. Two more submissions were rejected for free and excluded from the debrief counts.

## Frustration log

1. **Assignments and Orientation.** I saw “Fields and nested selections” on the first assignment and several more unfamiliar terms on the locked cards. I took Orientation because I couldn't tell how much the first case expected me to know. Orientation's query and JSON response clarified the request shape. The first card could describe something I will do in the case, such as following a shipment from port to country. The navigation also calls Documentation a tab before I can see where it lives, and in Training it is a disclosure below the response.
2. **Training, drills 4 and 5.** The geography gave me a way to sanity-check the nesting. The fifth drill's instructions made me reread which `borders` belonged to Portugal and which would belong to Spain. “Under `name`” gave me a position to copy before I had fully worked out that the second `borders` was a sibling of Spain's `name`. A small indented example marking the insertion point would connect the wording to the editor.
3. **Training drill 6 and later Documentation lookups.** I had to open the documentation below the terminal, then find Country and Currency separately. In missions the same pattern meant bouncing from a field's return type to another collapsed entry and back to the editor. This was useful the first time, but became clerical work when a path crossed four types. The field descriptions already explain when to use braces. Linking the return type directly to its entry would make those descriptions easier to use.
4. **The Vanishing Azulejos, first deduction.** “What each shipment contains” pointed me toward `inspection { finding }`. Documentation says `manifest` is what the shipper declared, so I asked for both. The form accepted the manifest alone as proof of what the crate held, and the debrief used the manifest as its reference. The story could say why a declaration is sufficient to rule out this crate. Later assessments really did need the inspection finding, so this distinction matters beyond the first case.
5. **The Vanishing Azulejos, travel order, then several later forms.** I cited the route through Angola and its complete border list. The form still said “Needs evidence,” although the three supporting facts were right there. The explanation below them said to choose the receiving country above. I tried Travel before seeing that requirement and stayed in place. Once I picked Angola the same citations worked. The first assessment repeated the pattern with Senegal. Put unfilled answer fields next to their claims and distinguish “Choose an answer” from “Needs evidence.”
6. **The Vanishing Azulejos, Luanda.** The useful intake row originally had a generic Cite button. After I chose the transfer order, that same row could cite into the chosen order's draft despite living outside the order in the result tree. I got both claims to Supported, but had to work out whether the intake row belonged in the draft at all. A short cue on the intake row could say that the label comes from the shipment and can support the selected order. The schema also offers a direct signer and a separate authorization log. I chose the direct signer and it worked, but the briefing gave me little basis to tell when the two matter.
7. **The Borrowed Chronometer, first timed terminal.** The blank editor made this feel like two new challenges at once. I had to write the whole request from memory and weigh whether to spend an hour exploring. I reused the shipment path from the case and the currency path from training. The clerk clearly explained that rejected syntax was free, and I had 17 hours left, so the pressure came from anticipating a penalty. A neutral, one-field example beside the blank editor would let me recover the starting shape without giving away the case.
8. **The Guest Register, ruling out sightings.** I could disprove a cover name directly, but to contradict the language claim I first had to select Mozambique as the country of a sighting I was trying to reject. That makes sense as a description of the sighting, but felt like entering an answer for a lead I intended to discard. Then I supported both claims on the surviving sighting and looked for Submit deduction before noticing Travel. A brief cue could tell me that choosing the reported country is part of testing a lead and that the supported sighting becomes a paid travel order.
9. **The Quiet Consignee, first assessment after learning arguments.** The briefing spent a paragraph preparing me for a field check. I wasn't sure whether this was a mandatory step or what to query when the obvious `consignee` was known to be blank. I asked for the intake, unloading receipt, and transfer orders together. The intake path proved both claims without a field check. A short clue distinguishing arrival records from shipping papers would help me investigate on purpose. Labeling the field check as a paid fallback would preserve the decision of whether to use it. I did not test the field-check outcome.
10. **Three Couriers, first alias query.** The starter asked me to add two people in one transmission. I copied the visible `operative` block with two different codenames and got a conflict. The clerk explained the duplicate response key and pointed to the Glossary. I added three distinct labels and the retry worked for free. This was a productive mistake. It would be easier to recover with the example beside the error, and the assignment could explicitly point out why the result needs separate keys. A player could also avoid this lesson by spending one hour per courier.
11. **Bound Ledgers and Lantern Export, reading large Findings panels.** Comparing the clues was satisfying, but it meant scanning repeated row labels and Cite controls across several deeply nested records. In Lantern Export, I had to keep track of which Arabic list went with which shipment and which end of its route. I followed the shipment IDs and the origin and destination paths. A compact comparison view with one row per shipment would make the puzzle easier to read while preserving the individual rows for citation.
12. **The Paymaster, first fragment.** The starter asks for the same dossier again down the chain. The glossary showed a one-field fragment and said fragments can spread other fragments. I tried putting `knownAssociates { ...Dossier }` inside `Dossier` and got “Cannot spread fragment … within itself.” The clerk gave a generic direction to check Documentation. The working query spelled out each level of `knownAssociates` and reused a small fragment inside those levels. A two-level example would explain what gets reused and where traversal stops. The directness clue could also say why I need the second layer. I found the intended decoy there: a person who uses Swiss francs but is an associate of an associate.
13. **The Forwarding Address, first variables.** I could copy the starter, but I found a contradiction once I checked Documentation. It still says “You never type a `!` in a query,” while the working starter declares `$city: String!`. The glossary says to copy the argument's type. I kept the starter's punctuation and updated only `city` in the JSON panel after travel. This was the first point where I could reproduce the syntax before I could explain which guidance applied. Give the clearance-five documentation an explicit exception for variable declarations and put the declaration, use, and JSON key in one annotated example.
14. **The Forwarding Address and The Registrar's Return, travel transition.** The query stays on the desk, and so does the old city in the JSON panel. I changed the value on purpose and the repeated query made the lesson click. A visible prompt near the panel would help a player who assumes traveling changes the city automatically. The first leg also puts Istanbul under Turkey and Turkey under Asia in the archive. I followed the returned country classification. Players who know that Istanbul spans two continents could hesitate unless the clue says to use the archive's country field.
15. **The Registrar's Return, final debrief.** My next clue added a director, so I changed both the city value and the query. The reference anticipated that clue and asked for the director earlier. The debrief explicitly called my way equally good, which removed the sting. This payoff would be stronger if the assessment let me reuse a clearly signposted variable pattern when the editor starts empty, especially after the conflicting rule about `!`.

## Pattern matching and replay value

By the end of training I could explain fields, lists, and nesting in terms of the JSON I got back. I briefly copied the position of the second `borders` before I could explain it. Aliases made sense as distinct JSON keys after the rejected request. Fragments were the biggest jump. I learned through a failed recursive version that a fragment supplies fields at a place I choose. At clearance five I could make variables work, but copied `String!` before I knew how to reconcile the documentation. Changing only the JSON city after travel finally demonstrated what was useful about the syntax.

I would play another case. The moments I remember are discovering that the similarly named companies played opposite roles, ruling out a hotel guest with NIGHTJAR's own records, and running the same query in a new city by changing one value. The loop felt least like an investigation when I'd found the answer and still had to discover what the form wanted. By the later cases, swapping in a new currency or language was starting to feel repetitive. The final note from M. made me curious about what happens next.

## Caveats

This was one simulated novice run. It shows where the on-screen path worked and where the interface or text made me stop. The simulation can assemble long queries faster than a new programmer and cannot measure real reading time or drop-off. I took the training path. I did not test skipping it, hints, field-check dispatches, deadlines, placement, mobile layouts, or keyboard-only play. I used efficient editor input through the browser and did not measure accidental bracket completion or typing errors. The findings above are logged for follow-up. This pass made no player-facing changes.
